The surface is the lapse field N(x,y) = 1/U(x,y;λ,ε), height-lifted as in the paper's (x,y,z,N) graph construction — not a new physical dimension, just a way to see the redshift field as a landscape. What you're looking at

The bifurcation

For the symmetric three-center family (M₁,M₂,M₃)=(1,1,λ), the finite critical-point structure of the lapse changes twice as λ increases: a symmetry-breaking pitchfork at λ₋ ≈ 0.6739 spawns an off-axis pair of S₁ saddles, and a fold at λ₊ ≈ 1.1363 annihilates an S₁/S₂ pair back down — the pattern 2S₁ → 3S₁+S₂ → 2S₁.

Why a landscape, not a contour plot

A contour or Reeb-graph view of the same field only tracks how level-set components merge. The gradient skeleton 𝒮ₙ = (C, I, V, Γ, ℬ) additionally encodes which critical points are saddles of which index (I), the separatrix/heteroclinic structure connecting them (Γ, the solid/dashed curves drawn here), and which source each region flows into (ℬ, the Basin coloring toggle) — all information a level-set summary alone discards. See the paper's comparison section for the full argument.

The cusp unfolding

Set ε≠0 and the two off-axis S₁ saddles born at the pitchfork no longer appear symmetrically at the same λ — the transition becomes a smooth fold whose location λ_fold(ε) drifts away from λ₋ as C|ε|^(2/3), the paper's validated cusp-scaling law. Sweep λ at a fixed ε≠0 to feel the fold replace the sharp pitchfork.

How this differs from the certified results

This page finds critical points and traces manifolds with a plain (uncertified) Newton solver and fixed-step integrator, for a smooth real-time picture. The repository's proof/ scripts certify the same bifurcation values and root counts rigorously, with outward-rounded interval arithmetic and arbitrary-precision ball arithmetic — see the repository for the machine-checked version of what this page shows interactively. The manifold/basin curves shown here are not certified at all — they're for intuition only.
